Card Offer Clarity

A denominator check changed the recommendation.

Mamkon Mercy Oyeleke · Data Analytics & Business Intelligence Consultant

First analyzed in a 2024 George Brown College group project; rebuilt independently in 2026.

The decision: fund an explainer video with a recap, remove taglines that add no information, and use one message across income groups.

The evidence: without taglines, correct choices rose from 42.5% with the plain video to 65.0% with the recap video. Overall taglines reduced correct choices (p = 0.0001); income showed no association (p = 0.97).

The condition: re-test with real customers and track the correct-choice rate after launch.

Fictional banking scenario. A regional bank's card team is reviewing its offer pages. The evidence comes from an online experiment, not that bank's customers. No real bank or commercial impact is claimed.

The result

Clearer information, better choices.

Each respondent chose among four card offers, one of which was the dominant, or best, card. Video type and tagline presence were randomized. Every rate below uses the respondents within that treatment group as its denominator.

Correct-choice rates by experimental treatment

Plain video (Baseline)None173 / 40742.5%
Plain videoSuperfluous145 / 39436.8%

Recap videoSuperfluous207 / 40551.1%

Taglines reduced correct choices overall (p = 0.0001); within the plain-video group alone the effect was not significant (p = 0.099).

The overall p-value is Pearson; the plain-video p-value is a logistic Wald test. The interaction p-value is approximately 0.101, so a difference in tagline effects between video groups was not established.

Income: no evidence for targeting

Correct-choice rates ranged from 47.4% to 51.1% across income groups. Pearson p = 0.9697. The earlier recommendation to target lower-income customers was unsupported by this analysis.

Card holding: an association

Cardholders chose correctly 46.4% of the time (554/1193), compared with 55.9% for nonholders (229/410); Pearson p = 0.0010. This does not show that card holding causes lower accuracy.

A clearer comparison for switchers is a hypothesis to test. Switching status and that mechanism were not measured.

Reporting quality

Four errors, traced to their causes.

I checked the original 2024 SAS code and analysis notes against independently recomputed results. The review distinguishes incorrect commentary from code that was already appropriate.

The work

Validate the logic before reporting the KPI.

  1. Define: one respondent per row; correct choices divided by all respondents in each group.
  2. Check: 1,603 unique IDs and 68 source variables. Flag three missing ages and one age of 200; retain every respondent.
  3. Reconcile: a reusable PROC SQL macro produces five grouped KPI tables. Exact-count checks stop processing if totals differ from 783 best choices among 1,603 respondents.
  4. Test: Pearson chi-square tests and logistic regression with a video-by-tagline interaction, checked independently in Python.
  5. Deliver: four survey fractions, 15 aggregate CSV exports, reviewed SAS logs, and a documented discrepancy review.

Survey checks: 128/1603 = 8.0%; 128/419 = 30.5%; 128/140 = 91.4%; 23/226 = 10.2%. Overall card holding was 1193/1603 = 74.4%.

Processing at scale

5,000,000 rows. A performance test.

I resampled respondents with replacement using seed 2026 and ran the resulting five-million-row table in SAS OnDemand for Academics. The program uses KEEP=, indexes with WHERE, PROC SUMMARY NWAY, a hash lookup, and OPTIONS FULLSTIMER.

All grouped, overall, and survey rates stayed within one percentage point of the real rates. The largest observed gap was 0.123520 percentage points. Synthetic rows add no new research evidence.

What this case can and cannot establish

  • US online participants, not bank customers; re-test with real customers before launch.
  • A single choice moment, with no long-term usage, fees paid, retention, or conversion outcomes.
  • Both groups watched a video; this does not test video versus no video.
  • Self-reported survey items. Card holding, sex, and income were not randomized; associations do not establish causes.
  • No significant income association does not prove equivalence or rule out every subgroup interaction.
  • Original code and analysis notes were reviewed; a historical rendered report was not inspected.
  • Synthetic resampling adds no evidence. No commercial return, production deployment, or Power BI implementation is claimed.
