Environmental decision research · Ontario

When can weather-based ozone estimates inform planning?

I studied afternoon ground-level ozone across 47 selected Ontario locations. I wanted to understand where modelled conditions differed, how well weather inputs helped estimate ozone on later days, and how much evidence a stakeholder would need before relying on an alert threshold.

Patterns in the data

I compared location-level models using temperature, wind and solar radiation. In the saved Toronto evaluation, the root mean squared error was 7.51 parts per billion on later days. The reference series is modelled ozone from the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service. The result measures agreement with that series and needs separate validation against station observations.

Impact and decision trade-offs

At the illustrative 60 parts per billion threshold, 46 of the 47 saved location holdouts had zero event days. That leaves too little evidence to estimate how often the model catches events across most locations. The research shows why average prediction error and alert performance need separate checks. The weather scenarios show how model estimates respond to different inputs under the fitted assumptions.

Who can use the research?

  • Public health teams can identify locations and periods where station validation or additional monitoring would strengthen an alert evaluation.
  • Municipal planners can review geographic coverage and identify gaps before comparing communities.
  • Schools and outdoor employers can use the research to understand the uncertainty behind model estimates and consult the official Air Quality Health Index for day-to-day guidance.

Methods and dashboard

I used Python for time alignment, regression, and chronological validation. I used Power BI to show location comparisons, uncertainty, and weather scenarios. The report presents a dated research snapshot.
